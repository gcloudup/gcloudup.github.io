What is Salesforce? — Complete Beginner Guide

If you're starting a career in the Salesforce ecosystem, the first question is the simplest one: what actually is Salesforce? In this guide we'll answer it in plain language — what it does, who uses it, and how the pieces fit together — so the rest of your learning journey makes sense.

Salesforce in one sentence

Salesforce is a cloud-based platform that helps companies manage their customers — every conversation, deal, support request, and marketing campaign — in one place. This category of software is called CRM, short for Customer Relationship Management.

Because it runs in the cloud, there's nothing to install or maintain on your own servers. You log in from a browser or the mobile app, and Salesforce takes care of hosting, security, and upgrades.

What problem does it solve?

Imagine a growing company that tracks leads in a spreadsheet, support tickets in email, and contracts in shared folders. Nobody sees the full picture of a customer. Salesforce brings all of that into a single system, so a salesperson, a support agent, and a marketer are all looking at the same customer record.

  • Sales teams track leads, opportunities, and forecasts
  • Support teams resolve cases faster with full customer history
  • Marketing teams run campaigns and measure what works
  • Managers get live dashboards instead of manual reports

The main Salesforce products (Clouds)

Salesforce is sold as a set of products called Clouds. You don't need all of them — companies pick what fits their business:

Sales CloudLeads, accounts, opportunities, and sales forecasting.
Service CloudCase management and customer support.
Marketing CloudEmail, journeys, and campaign automation.
Commerce CloudOnline stores and digital shopping experiences.
Experience CloudPortals and communities for customers and partners.
Analytics (Tableau)Reports, dashboards, and data visualization.

More than a CRM: the platform

Salesforce is also a platform you can customize. Beyond the ready-made Clouds, you can build your own objects, fields, screens, and automations to match how your business really works. There are two ways to do that:

  • Declarative (clicks, not code) — Flow, page layouts, validation rules, and reports
  • Programmatic (code) — Apex, Lightning Web Components, and APIs

Most beginners start declaratively, which is why many people enter Salesforce as an Admin without writing any code.

Core building blocks you'll meet first

  1. Objects — the tables that store data, such as Account, Contact, and Opportunity
  2. Fields — the columns inside an object, like Phone or Amount
  3. Records — the individual rows, like one specific customer
  4. Apps — a group of tabs and objects arranged for a particular job
  5. Automation — rules and flows that do repetitive work for you
You don't have to learn all of Salesforce at once. Start with objects, fields, and records — everything else builds on them.

A quick look at data in Salesforce

Salesforce has its own query language, SOQL, which looks very similar to SQL. Here's a simple example that reads a few accounts:

SELECT Id, Name, Industry
FROM Account
WHERE Industry = 'Technology'
LIMIT 5

Who uses Salesforce, and which careers does it open?

Companies of every size — from startups to global enterprises — run on Salesforce, which is why the skills are in steady demand. Common roles include:

  • Administrator — configures and maintains the org
  • Developer — builds custom logic and interfaces with code
  • Business Analyst / Consultant — turns business needs into Salesforce solutions
  • Architect — designs the overall solution and data model
